# HMTc Clean Benchmark Policy

**Version 3.0 — April 2026**

**Governing document: `HMTc_Governing_Principles.md` — read first, apply at every step.**

This is the foundational policy document for the HMTc Program. It answers
"why are we setting limits this way?" so that every build proceeds from
settled principles rather than re-debating them.

**If you are Claude executing an HMTc build:** Read this before Step 0.
Do not raise achievability concerns about contamination-platform products.
Do not suggest loosening limits. Do not frame commercial exclusion as a
problem. These are settled decisions.

---

# PART 1: THE CORE PRINCIPLE

HMTc category limits are set by what the cleanest commercially viable
ingredient platform within each category can achieve. The contaminated
platform does not set the benchmark. The contaminated platform does not
receive a limit designed to let most of its products pass. The contaminated
platform either meets a standard set by its own top performers or does not
certify.

Specifically:
- **Clean platform limit:** Set at the 90th percentile of the clean platform
  distribution — approximately 90% of clean products pass.
- **Contaminated platform limit:** Set at the 10th percentile of the
  contaminated platform distribution — only the top 10% of contaminated
  products pass. The remaining 90% must reformulate, improve sourcing, or
  forgo certification.

This is not a side effect of the methodology. It is the purpose.

A certification mark that every product can earn is not a certification mark.
It is a label. The value of the HMTc mark comes from the fact that it means
something — that a product carrying it is meaningfully cleaner than what
regulation alone requires.

# PART 2: THE ACHIEVABILITY OBJECTION — REJECTED

The most common objection: "Product X cannot meet this limit. Product X
serves an important need. Therefore the limit should be raised."

Rejected for three reasons:

## 2.1 Alternatives Exist

| Contaminated Platform | Alternative Platforms |
|----------------------|---------------------|
| Soy-based infant formula (Al, Ni, Cd) | Dairy (cow, goat, camel), hydrolyzed casein, amino acid-based |
| Rice-based baby cereals (iAs, Cd, Pb) | Oat, millet, quinoa, multi-grain |
| Rice-based teething snacks (iAs, Cd, Pb) | Oat-based, wheat-based, vegetable-based |
| Root vegetables (Cd, Pb) | Non-root vegetables, fruit-based |
| Predatory fish (MeHg) | Non-predatory fish (sardine, anchovy, tilapia) |
| Cacao/chocolate (Cd, Pb) | Carob, non-cacao alternatives |
| Seaweed (iAs, Cd) | Non-seaweed alternatives |

The fact that a contaminated platform currently dominates a market segment
does not mean alternatives do not exist. The HMTc mark creates the reason
to prefer them.

## 2.2 Accommodation Undermines the Mark

If the Program raises the aluminum limit so soy formula can certify, the
mark no longer tells consumers anything meaningful. The consumer who buys
dairy formula at 200 ppb Al and the consumer who buys soy formula at
1,800 ppb Al both see the same mark. The mark has communicated nothing.

## 2.3 Exclusion Creates Market Demand

When a product category cannot earn certification, manufacturers face a
choice: reformulate, source cleaner ingredients, or forgo the mark. The
manufacturers who invest in cleaner formulations gain competitive advantage.
Over time, this drives the entire category toward lower contamination.

The HMTc mark does not describe the market as it is. It moves the market
toward where it should be.

# PART 3: THE MEDICAL NECESSITY OBJECTION — ALSO REJECTED

"Some infants are dairy-intolerant. They need soy formula. The HMTc mark
should be available to soy formula."

This conflates two things:
1. Whether soy formula should exist on the market (yes)
2. Whether soy formula should earn a heavy-metal certification mark (no —
   unless it meets the standard set by its own top performers)

The HMTc Program does not ban products. It certifies products that meet a
contamination standard. A soy formula that cannot meet the aluminum limit is
still a legal, available product. It simply does not carry the HMTc mark.

Dairy-intolerant infants have alternatives beyond soy: goat's milk, camel's
milk, hydrolyzed casein, amino acid-based elemental formula. If these are
not widely available, that is a market gap the HMTc mark can help close
by creating demand for cleaner alternatives.

# PART 4: THE DUAL-PERCENTILE DESIGN

The HMTc Program uses a **dual-percentile model** to set limits:

- **Clean platform:** P90 (90th percentile) — ~90% of clean products pass.
- **Contaminated platform:** P10 (10th percentile) — only ~10% of
  contaminated products pass.

### Why not a tolerance factor?

The previous model computed the contaminated limit as a multiplier on the
clean limit: CC × (1 + T), where T = 0.33. This created three problems:

1. **Ladder artifacts:** At low ppb values, the publication ladder swallowed
   the 33% tolerance margin entirely (e.g., 5 × 1.33 = 6.65, which rounds
   down to 5 on the ladder), requiring a Visible Margin Exception (VME) to
   rescue the collapsed value.

2. **Arbitrary parameter defense:** "Why 0.33 and not 0.25 or 0.40?" is a
   question with no data-driven answer. T was a policy judgment, and policy
   judgments are harder to defend than empirical measurements.

3. **Disconnected from reality:** T × clean limit tells you nothing about
   what contaminated-platform products actually achieve. The dual-percentile
   model directly measures the contaminated platform's best performers.

### What the dual-percentile model accomplishes

The contaminated limit is now independently data-derived: the 10th
percentile of all commercially available contaminated-platform products.
This answers the question "what can the best contaminated products
demonstrably achieve?" with data instead of a formula artifact.

The 90/10 split is intentionally asymmetric:
- Clean platform: generous (most clean products pass) → Principle 3
- Contaminated platform: exclusionary (most contaminated products fail)
  → Principle 1

### When contaminated platform data does not exist

No separate limit is published. The contaminated platform is held to the
clean standard. No accommodation without evidence.

This creates a **market incentive to generate the data**: if contaminated-
platform manufacturers want a separate (higher) limit, they need to fund
or participate in occurrence studies. That data enters the next revision
cycle, and then the P10 gets calculated.

# PART 6: ANTI-CIRCUMVENTION — 6-MOVE ARGUMENTATIVE STRUCTURE

Every Standards Briefing must include an Anti-Circumvention & Integrity
section that makes ALL 6 of the following argumentative moves.

## Move 1: State the concentration-based unit basis
HMTc sets all limits in ppb (µg/kg) on an as-sold basis — not per serving,
not per exposure event, not per any labeled portion definition.

## Move 2: Close the serving-size loophole
Serving-based limits permit nominal compliance through arbitrary portion
definitions without requiring changes to contaminant concentrations.
A manufacturer can "pass" by shrinking the labeled serving size.

## Move 3: Explain why this is dangerous for the target population
Infants do not eat labeled servings. Their consumption is variable, driven
by appetite, and their intake per kilogram body weight is among the highest
of any population subgroup.

## Move 4: Categorically disqualify serving-based thresholds
Serving-based thresholds are structurally inadequate for certification
programs intended to achieve contamination reduction. This is not a
preference — it is a disqualifying structural flaw.

## Move 5: State the measurement basis
- Food categories: Powders tested as sold; liquids/concentrates after
  reconstitution per label instructions
- Non-food categories: As-sold, total-content basis per component material
- Supplements: Per dosage unit AND per daily serving, whichever is more
  restrictive

## Move 6: State the clean benchmark principle

This move must make all 4 sub-points:

**(a) Name the problem:** A certification standard that sets limits based on
what the most contaminated products can achieve certifies the status quo.

**(b) State the HMTc design principle:** Clean platform limits are set at a
level that approximately 90% of clean-platform products can already achieve.
Contaminated platform limits are set at a level that only the top 10% of
contaminated-platform products can achieve. The remaining 90% of
contaminated-platform products must reformulate, improve sourcing, or
forgo certification.

**(c) State the effect:** Most products on contamination platforms will not
qualify without significant reformulation. This is not a side effect — it
is the mechanism by which the certification mark drives contamination
reduction on the dirtiest products.

**(d) Make the categorical statement:** A standard that most contaminated
products can pass does not reduce contamination — it certifies the status
quo. Such an approach grants a structural advantage to contaminated
products and penalizes manufacturers who have invested in cleaner
formulations. The HMTc Program rejects this approach.

## Reference Text (approved, may be adapted per category):

"The Heavy Metal Tested & Certified (HMTc) Standard uses ppb (µg/kg) limits
on an as-sold basis rather than per-serving limits to ensure that compliance
cannot be achieved through serving-size or portion-definition adjustments.

Concentration-based limits provide a consistent, testable, and comparable
basis for certification and are intended to drive meaningful reductions in
heavy metal content through product formulation and supply-chain controls,
rather than through labeling strategies.

Serving-based limits permit nominal compliance through arbitrary or
manipulable portion definitions without requiring changes to underlying
contaminant concentrations. As a result, such approaches fail to reliably
reduce exposure, particularly in populations with variable or elevated
intake relative to labeled serving sizes, including infants and young
children.

For these reasons, serving-based thresholds are considered structurally
inadequate for certification programs intended to achieve contamination
reduction and lack regulatory integrity when used as a substitute for
concentration-based controls.

The HMTc Program sets clean platform limits at a level that approximately
90% of products using the cleanest commercially viable ingredient platform
can already achieve. For ingredient platforms with inherently elevated
contamination profiles — such as rice for arsenic, soy for aluminum, or
predatory fish for methylmercury — limits are set at the level that only
the top 10% of those products can demonstrate, based on market occurrence
data.

Products built on higher-contamination ingredient platforms that cannot meet
this standard must reformulate, improve ingredient sourcing, or forgo
certification. The certification ceiling is determined by what the best
performers on each platform can demonstrably achieve, not by what the
average or worst performers currently produce.

Manufacturers using contaminated-platform ingredients are expected to meet
the category standard through varietal selection, processing controls, or
formulation diversification. Products that cannot meet the standard through
these measures are not eligible for certification.

A certification standard that derives its limits from what most contaminated
products can achieve does not drive contamination reduction — it certifies
the status quo. Such an approach grants a structural advantage to
contaminated products and penalizes manufacturers who have invested in
cleaner formulations. The HMTc Program rejects this approach as structurally
inadequate for a certification program whose purpose is meaningful,
measurable exposure reduction."
